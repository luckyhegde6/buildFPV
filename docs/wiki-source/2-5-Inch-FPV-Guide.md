# 2.5-Inch FPV Quads — Purpose, Parts, Trade-offs and First Setup

![Sub250 OasisFly25 2.5-inch frame product photo](https://www.unmannedtechshop.co.uk/cdn/shop/files/Sub250-Oasisfly25-Frame-2.webp?v=1753832978&width=1946)

*Real frame photo: Sub250 OasisFly25. The manufacturer lists a 135 mm wheelbase, 2.5-inch props and HQ2.5×2.5×3 propeller model on its current product listing. [Sub250 specification](https://sub250.com/en-nl/collections/oasisfly-series/products/sub250-oasisfly25-hd-o3). Image hosted by Unmanned Tech.*

A 2.5-inch FPV build uses propellers around **63.5 mm nominal diameter**. Its frame wheelbase is a different dimension, and propellers/clearance, all-up weight, battery, camera/video gear and flight purpose matter more than the category name alone.

## 1. What is a 2.5-inch quad good for?

Common goals are portable outdoor practice, lightweight freestyle, travel builds, and compact filming with small camera/video hardware. Some are called **toothpick** builds (light, open propellers); some are more reinforced or protected. “2.5-inch” tells you nominal propeller diameter, not whether the aircraft is a cinewhoop or a toothpick.

**Strengths:** compact, potentially lighter and less energy-intensive than larger quads; some models support capable HD video; easier to pack and store.

**Trade-offs:** exposed propellers still pose injury/damage risk; small batteries and frames leave little room for payload; wind affects light builds; adding an HD air unit or action camera can substantially change mass, current draw and handling.

## 3. Open prop, toothpick or ducts?

- **Open-prop micro/toothpick:** lower guard mass and less duct drag in many designs; exposed props require a carefully chosen flying location and clearance.
- **Guarded / cine-style micro:** may reduce some prop-to-object contact risk and offer camera mounting, but guards add mass and drag. They do not make the quad safe around people.
- **HD payload:** camera/air-unit weight and power draw may be significant on a 2.5-inch build. Confirm frame mounting, airflow, supported input voltage, camera dimensions and suitable battery.

See [Tinywhoops and Micro FPV](Tinywhoop-and-Micro-FPV-Guide.md) and [3.5-Inch Cinewhoop Guide](3-5-Inch-Cinewhoop-Guide.md).

## 4. Match frame, motor, prop, ESC and battery

Do not choose a motor just because it says 1404/4500KV or because the frame is called 2.5-inch. Confirm the exact maker's voltage/prop table and:
- Motor mount/shaft and screw length; no motor screw may touch winding wire.
- Prop diameter, pitch, blade count and frame/duct clearance.
- Battery series count/full voltage, connector polarity, dimensions and current rating.
- ESC voltage/current capability for the tested motor + prop + battery combination.
- FC/receiver, camera/video unit voltage, cooling and mounting.
- Actual all-up mass with battery, antennas, camera mounts and guards.

Use [Motors, Propellers and Battery Compatibility](Motors-Propellers-and-Battery-Compatibility.md), not a universal KV chart.

## 5. Setup and first-flight checklist

- [ ] Confirm exact BNF/PNP/DIY included items and receiver protocol.
- [ ] Confirm camera, VTX/air unit, antenna and goggles/VRX compatibility.
- [ ] Verify frame drawing: wheelbase, motor holes, stack holes, camera space and battery.
- [ ] Inspect soldering, polarity and connectors; use manufacturer manuals.
- [ ] Remove props for firmware, receiver, motor order/direction and failsafe tests.
- [ ] Confirm failsafe/arming and battery limits before prop installation.
- [ ] Test in a suitable permitted location with ample distance from people and property.
